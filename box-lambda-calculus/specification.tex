\section{\texorpdfstring{Specification of \boldmath${\la_b}$}{ Specification of the call-by-box lambda calculus}}

\subsection{Intuitionistic logic IS4}
Intuitionistic logic \textsf{IS4} is a non-classical logic that is based on the idea of constructive proof.
Unlike classical logic, intuitionistic logic does not allow for the law of excluded middle, which states that every proposition is either \texttt{true} or \texttt{false}.
Intuitionistic logic also does not allow for the law of double negation, which states that if a proposition is not \texttt{false}, then it is \texttt{true}.
Thus, if we know that proposition $A$ not \texttt{false}, we cannot conclude that it is \texttt{true}.
In intuitionistic logic, a proposition is only considered \texttt{true} if it can be constructively proven.
That is, a proposition is \texttt{true} if we can build a proof step by step, much like writing a program.
This is why programming languages and proofs in systems like Agda and Coq are based on intuitionistic logic.
Since lambda calculus is the foundation of functional programming, it corresponds with intuitionistic logic via the Curry-Howard correspondence,
where programs are equivalent to proofs and types to propositions.

The intuitionistic modal logic \textsf{IS4} is an extension of intuitionistic logic that introduces \emph{modal operators}.
The \labox calculus is a fragment of \textsf{IS4} that, besides the usual \lterms, also uses box ($\mathsf{box}$) and unbox ($\varepsilon$) operators 
and is strong enough to interpret both call-by-name (\textsf{cbn}) and call-by-value (\textsf{cbv}) lambda calculi \cite{espritosanto_et_al:LIPIcs:2019:10525}.
The box is like a little container that delays a computation, making it possible to simulate both \textsf{cbn} and \textsf{cbv} evaluation strategies. 
In this research, we formalize the \emph{boxed lambda calculus} in Agda, denoted as \lab, which is as a refinement of the simpler \labox calculus.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Terms and Types of \boldmath${\la_b}$}{ Terms and Types of the boxed lambda calculus}}
We now define the types of the \lab calculus. We indicate the types of \lab with $A$ and $B$, representing types and boxed types respectively.
The type $X$ denotes the base type. Note that functions always take boxed types as input.

\begin{definition}[Types of \lab]
  \label{def:typesb}
	\begin{align*}
    \begin{grammar}{
      \pr{$A$}{$X \gors B \fa A \gors B$}
    } \quad \quad
    \end{grammar}
    \begin{grammar}{
      \pr{$B$}{$\boxit{A}$}
    }
    \end{grammar}
  \end{align*}

\end{definition}


We give some examples of types in \lab. Note that $A$ can be any type that is not boxed, while $B$ can be any type that is boxed. 
The type $X$ is the base type. 

\begin{example}[Types in \lab]
  \label{ex:typesb}
  % The following types are valid in \lab:
  \begin{align*}
    & X \\
    & \boxt{(B \fa A)} \\
    & \boxt{X} \fa X \\
    & \boxt{A} \fa \boxt{B} \\
    & \boxt{\boxt{\boxt{X}}} \fa \boxt{X}
  \end{align*}
\end{example}

We now define the terms of \lab. The terms are defined by the grammar in \ref{def:lab}.

\begin{definition}[Terms in \lab]
  \label{def:lab}
  % The terms of \lab are given by: \\
  \begin{align*}    
    \begin{grammar}{
      \pr{$M, N, P, Q$}{$\boxe{x} \gors \lambda x.M \gors M N \gors \boxi{N}$}
    }
    \end{grammar}
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

We give some examples of terms that are well typed in \lab. 

\begin{example}[Terms in \lab]
  \label{ex:termsb}
  % The following terms are valid in \lab:
  Assuming \dots, the following terms are well-typed in \lab:
  \begin{align*}
    & \boxe{x} \\
    & \lamb{x}{\boxe{x}} \\
    & (\lamb{x}{\boxe{x}}) \boxi{\boxe{y}} \\
    & (\lamb{x}{\boxe{x}}) \boxi{(\lamb{y}{\boxe{y}})}
  \end{align*}
\end{example}

We now define the typing rules of \lab. The Id$_\square$ and $\square$-I rule may need some explanation.
The $\square$-I rule states that if $M$ is of type $A$, then $\boxi{M}$ is of type $\boxt{A}$.
The Id$_\square$ rule is the identity rule for boxed variables, which states that if we know that $x$ is a variable of type $\boxt{A}$, we can conclude that $\boxe{x}$ is of type $A$.
Due to the grammar of \lab, we can only have variables of boxed types which is why Id$_\square$ is defined as such.


\begin{definition}
  The typing rules of \lab are give by:
  \begin{prooftree}
    \def\extraVskip{5pt}
    \AxiomC{\phantom{A}}
    \RightLabel{Id$_\square$}
    \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma, x : \boxt{A}}{\boxe{x} : A}$} % Conclusion
  \end{prooftree}
  
  \begin{prooftree}
    \def\extraVskip{5pt}
    \AxiomC{$\judgement{\Gamma, x : A}{M : B}$}
    \RightLabel{\impliesi}
    \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma}{\lamb{x}{M} : A \fa B}$} % Conclusion
  \end{prooftree}
  
  \begin{prooftree}
    \def\extraVskip{5pt}
    \AxiomC{$\judgement{\Gamma}{M : A \fa B}$}
    \AxiomC{$\judgement{\Delta}{N : A}$}
    \RightLabel{\impliese}
    \BinaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma, \Delta}{M N : B}$} % Conclusion
  \end{prooftree}
  
  \begin{prooftree}
    \def\extraVskip{5pt}
    \AxiomC{$\judgement{\Gamma}{M : A}$}
    \RightLabel{$\square$-I}
    \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma}{\boxi{M} : \boxt{A}}$} % Conclusion
  \end{prooftree}
\end{definition}

\section{\texorpdfstring{Substitution and Free and Bound variables in ${\la_b}$}{ Substitution and Free and Bound variables in the boxed lambda calculus}}

Since variables always occur inside $\varepsilon$, we need to redefine free and bound variables and substitution.
For \lab, we have: 

\begin{definition}[Free and Bound Variables]
  \label{def:fv}
  The free and bound variables of a term $M$ are defined by:
  \begin{align*}
    & FV_b(\boxe{x})    & &= \{\boxe{x}\}                   & & BV(\boxe{x})    & &= \emptyset \\
    & FV_b(\lamb{x}{M}) & &= FV(M) \backslash \{\boxe{x}\}  & & BV(\lamb{x}{M}) & &= BV(M) \cup \{\boxe{x}\} \\
    & FV_b(M N)         & &= FV(M) \cup FV(N)               & & BV(M N)         & &= BV(M) \cup BV(N)
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Substitution in \lab]
  \label{def:subst}
  The substitution of a term $Q$ for a variable $\boxe{x}$ in a term $M$ is defined as follows:
  \[
\begin{aligned}
	& \boxe{x} \subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}            & &= Q \\
	& \boxe{y}  \subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}           & &= \boxe{y} \text{ if } (x \neq y)\\
  & \boxi{M} \subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}            & &= \boxi{M \subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}} \\
	& (M N) \subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}               & &= M \subst{x}{Q} N \subst{x}{Q} \\
	& (\lamb{x}{M}) \subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}       & &= \lamb{x}{M} \\
	& (\lamb{y}{M}) \subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}       & &= \lamb{z}{M \subst{\boxe{y}}{\boxe{z}}\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}} \text{ if } (x \neq y) \\
\end{aligned}
\]
with $z$ a variable defined by:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item%1
	If $\boxe{x} \notin FV_b(N)$ or $\boxe{y} \notin FV_b(M)$ then $z = y$
	\item%2
	Otherwise, $z$ can be any variable such that $\boxe{z} \notin FV(N)$ or $\boxe{z} \notin FV(M)$
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}


With this new lambda calculus, it may not be completely clear that substitution preserves well-typedness.
We can prove that substitution preserves well-typedness in \lab.
\begin{proposition}[Preservation of well-typedness]
  \[
    \judgement{\Gamma, x : \boxt{A}}{M : B} \mbox{ and } \judgement{\Gamma}{Q : A} \implies \judgement{\Gamma}{M \subst{\boxe{x}}{Q} : B}
  \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  We prove by structural induction on the term $M$ that this substitution preserves well-typedness.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item%1 
    \[
    \boxe{x}\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q} = Q
    \]
    
    Since $Q$ is well-typed, and $Q$ is the result of the substitution, we have that well-typedness is preserved.
    \item%2
    \[
    \boxe{y}\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q} = \boxe{y} \quad \text{if} \ x \neq y
    \]
  
    We have that $x \neq y$, and the result of the substitution is $\boxe{y}$, which is well-typed.
    
    \item%3
    \[
    \boxi{M}\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q} = \boxi{M\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}}
    \]
    We have that $\boxi{M}$ is well-typed and thus $M$ is well-typed because of the $\square$-I rule.
    By the inductive hypothesis, we have that $M\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}$ is well-typed.
    We also have that $\boxi{M\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}}$ is well-typed because of the $\square$-I rule.
    Thus, we have that $\boxi{M}\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}$ is well-typed.

    \item%4
    \[
    (M N)\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q} = (M\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}) \ (N\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q})
    \]
  
    We have that $M N$ is well-typed and thus $M$ and $N$ are well-typed.
    By the inductive hypothesis, we get that $M\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}$ is well-typed, and $N\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}$ is well-typed.
    We also have that application of two well-typed terms is well-typed, so we have that $(M N)\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}$ is well-typed.
  
    \item%5
    \[
    (\lamb{x}{M})\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q} = \lamb{x}{M}
    \]
  
    We have that $\lamb{x}{M}$ is well-typed and thus $M$ is well-typed.
    Since $\boxe{x}$ is a bound variable in $\lamb{x}{M}$, no substitution occurs and the result is the well-typed term $\lamb{x}{M}$.
  
    \item%6
    \[
    (\lamb{y}{M})\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q} = \lamb{z}{(M\subst{\boxe{y}}{\boxe{z}}\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q})} \quad \text{if} \ x \neq y
    \]
  
    We have that $\lamb{y}{M}$ is well-typed and thus $M$ is well-typed.
    By the inductive hypothesis, we have that $M\subst{\boxe{y}}{\boxe{z}}$ and $M\subst{\boxe{y}}{\boxe{z}}\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q}$ are well-typed.
    We also have that abstraction of a well-typed term is well-typed, so we have that \\
    $\lamb{z}{(M\subst{\boxe{y}}{\boxe{z}}\subst{\boxe{x}}{Q})}$ is well-typed.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

With this definition of substitution, we define the reduction rule we can use for this \lab.

\begin{definition}[$\bb$]
  \label{def:beta}
  In \lab, $\beta$ reduction is defined as follows:
  \begin{align*}
    (\lamb{x}{M})\boxi{N} \red M \subst{\boxe{x}}{N} \quad \quad (\bb)
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

In \lab, the arguments of abstractions are always boxed, so we can only apply abstractions to boxed terms, 
which makes sense since the parameters like $x$ are always boxed. 
Since variables only occur insides $\varepsilon$, we can replace those with the corresponding unboxed term.

We define three relations: $\bb$ closed under $\mu$ and $\nu$, $\bb$ closed under $\mu$ and $\bb$ closed under $\nu$.
We call them $\redwe$, $\redbn$ and $\redbv$ respectively. 
The relation $\redwe$ is weak since values do not reduce.
That is, abstractions and terms of the form $\boxe{x}$ do not reduce.
The relation $\redwe$ is also external as boxes do not reduce. This relation is called call-by-box (cbb) evaluation.
We will see after defining the embeddings that $\redwe$ collapses to $\redbn$ in Girard's embedding and that $\redwe$ collapses to $\redbv$ in Gödel's embedding.
