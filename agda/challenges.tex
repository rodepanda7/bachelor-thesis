\section{Challenges of the Formalisation}
This section discusses the challenges of the formalisation of \lab in Agda.
The main challenge is dealing with variables and substitution.
For variables, we used de Bruijn indices, which are a way to represent variables without names.
The definition of substitution is based on the definition of substitution in the PLFA book \cite{plfa22.08},
and is based on the definition by McBride \cite{BENTON2012}.
The formalisation of \lab in Agda is restricted to well-typed terms, which avoids handling ill-typed terms.
Another challenge is explicit weakening and renaming, which is needed to handle the contexts of \lterms correctly.
Two \lterms with slightly different contexts are not the same, and we need to take this into consideration in the formalisation.

\subsection{De Bruijn Indices}
De Bruijn indices, introduced by De Bruijn, are a method to eliminate the need for variable names in the \lc \cite{DEBRUIJN1972381}.
This is useful because now all \lterms that are \aeqt are the same. 
We do not have to worry about variables with the same name that refer to different variables, or about variable capture, simplifying the formalisation in Agda.
The definition of substitution becomes simpler because of this.
This subsection first explains the concept of de Bruijn indices and then shows how they are implemented in the formalisation.

The \lterms that use de Bruijn indices do not use variable names, but integers. We precede the integers with \# to prevent confusion if the index is higher than 9.
Without \#, $10$ could be confused with two indices $1$ and $0$ that occur next to each other. 
These integers represent the "distance" to the corresponding $\lambda$. 
\Cref{ex:deBruijn-closed} gives representations of \lterms without free variables, using de Bruijn indices.
These terms are said to be closed.
Note that identical indices do not mean that the variables are the same. The \lterm $\lamb{}{\#0}\lamb{}{\#0}$ in \cref{ex:deBruijn-closed} demonstrates this.
In addition, variables that are the same can have different indices, which we demonstrate later.

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:deBruijn-closed}
  \textbf{De Bruijn indices for closed \lterms:}
  \begin{align*}
    \lamb{x}{x}                                  &\equiv \lamb{}{\#0}                         \\
    \lamb{x}{\lamb{y}{x}}                        &\equiv \lamb{}{\lamb{}{\#1}}                \\
    \lamb{x}{\lamb{y}{y}}                        &\equiv \lamb{}{\lamb{}{\#0}}                \\
    \lamb{x}{x}\lamb{y}{y}                       &\equiv \lamb{}{\#0}\lamb{}{\#0}            
  \end{align*}
\end{example}

Representing open \lterms (\lterms with free variables) using de Bruijn indices is a little more complex. 
First, we represent the \lterm with de Bruijn indices, ignoring all free variables. The idea here is that we wrap the entire \lterm inside an imaginary $\lambda$ that binds the first free variable, 
and we give it an index the same way as we do for bound variables.
We keep adding these imaginary $\lambda$s until we have no more free variables left if we consider this wrapped \lterm.
\Cref{ex:deBruijn-open} illustrates this, with the final \lterm being more complex. It is the same example that de Bruijn presented in his paper \cite{DEBRUIJN1972381}. 

The last term in \cref{ex:deBruijn-open} may need some explanation.
We first wrap the \lterm with three imaginary $\lambda$s, corresponding to $z$, $w$ and $y$ respectively. 
The $\lambda$ that binds $z$ is the outermost $\lambda$.
The indices \#2 and \#3 both refer to $w$, because index 3 is one $\lambda$ deeper, and
the de Bruijn indices can be seen as the distance between the corresponding $\lambda$ and the variable.
\begin{example}
  \label{ex:deBruijn-open}
  \textbf{De Bruijn indices for open \lterms:}
  \begin{align*}
    x\lamb{y}{y}                                                    &\equiv x\lamb{}{\#0}      \equiv \#0\lamb{}{\#0}           \\
    x\lamb{y}{x}                                                    &\equiv x\lamb{}{x}        \equiv \#0\lamb{}{\#1}           
  \end{align*}
  \begin{align*}
    \lamb{x}({\lamb{t}({x t (\lamb{u}{u t z})(\lamb{s}{w})})wy})    &\equiv \lamb{}({\lamb{}({\#1 \#0 (\lamb{}{\#0 \#1 z})(\lamb{}{w})})wy}) \\
                                                                    &\equiv \lamb{}({\lamb{}({\#1 \#0 (\lamb{}{\#0 \#1 \#5})(\lamb{}{\#3})})\#2\#1})    
  \end{align*} 
\end{example}

In the formalisation, we will represent de Bruijn indices similar to how natural numbers are represented in Agda.
De Bruijn index 0 is represented as \textsf{Z}, de Bruijn index 1 as \textsf{S Z}, de Bruijn index 2 as \textsf{S (S Z)}, and so on.


\subsection{Well-typed Terms}

This subsection discusses the restriction to well-typed terms in the formalisation of \lab.
The restriction to well-typed terms is important because it avoids all complications that arise from ill-typed terms. 
In particular, in the context of evaluation, it makes sense to restrict to well-typed terms, 
because ill-typed terms do not have a normal form.
Evaluation is only defined for well-typed terms, and thus the restriction to well-typed terms makes sense.

The formalisation uses an intrinsically typed approach where terms are defined together with their types.
In the formalisation, \lterms are automatically associated with a type, and we can only construct well-typed terms.
This approach is also referred to as Church style typing, and was introduced by Alonzo Church in \cite{ALONZOCHURCH1940}.

The formalisation is based on the representation proposed by AltenKirch and Reus in \cite{THORSTENALTENKIRCH1999}.
We briefly go over the types and \lterms in the formalisation, and how they are defined.

\subsection{Substitution}
The next challenge in the formalisation is a correct definition of substitution.
This definition appeared to be the most complex part of the formalisation, and it caused the most problems with the proofs later in this thesis.
The definition of substitution is similar to the definition that is used in the chapter ``De Bruijn indices'' in the PLFA book \cite{plfa22.08}.
It is based on the definition by McBride \cite{BENTON2012}, which gives a similar definition of substitution in Rocq.

We only give the definition of substitution in Agda of \lterms in \lab, since the definition of substitution in the standard \lc is exactly the same as the definition in \cite{plfa22.08}.
\Cref{def:substitution:lab} gives the definition of parallel substitution, which is the substitution of a list of variables by a list of \lterms.
Then we define single substitution in \cref{def:single:substitution:lab}, which might be more familiar and is the substitution of a single \lterm by another \lterm.

In general, substitution is about replacing variables by \lterms. With parallel substitution, multiple variables are replaced by other \lterms. 
So, substitution can be seen as a function, taking a map from variables in one context to \lterms in another context
and a \lterm as arguments, and returning a new \lterm with the variables replaced by the corresponding \lterms.

For the definition of parallel substitution, we need to have an extension lemma, which is needed when we encounter an abstraction.
An abstraction adds a new variable to the context, and we need a way to use the substitution function in the new context.
This is where the extension lemma \textsf{extsb} comes in.
It is a function from a map from variables in one context to \lterms in another context,
to a map from variables in the first context extended with a type to \lterms in the second context extended with a type.
The type that is used for extending the context is the input type of the $\lambda$ abstraction.
We can think of \textsf{extsb} as a way to apply some mapping function to the body of a $\lambda$ abstraction
while keeping the parameter of the abstraction untouched.

The extension lemma is defined in terms of \textsf{renameb}, which is used to construct a \lterm with an extended context.
We can think of \textsf{renameb} as a function that ``renames'' variables. We can change the context and adjust the de Bruijn indices accordingly.
In de case of substitution, we ``rename'' the \lterm by adding the input parameter of the $\lambda$ abstraction to the context and by increasing all de Bruijn indices by one.
The \textsf{renameb} function takes a function from variables in one context to variables in another context,
and returns a function from \lterms in the first context to \lterms in the second context.
For example, \textsf{renameb Sb $M$} renames $M$ by applying the function \textsf{Sb} to the variables in $M$.
An example of this is given in \cref{ex:rename:agda}.

The rename lemma is defined in terms of \textsf{extb}, which works similarly to \textsf{extsb}, but is only used for extending the context.
We can think of \textsf{extb} as a way to extend the context. Given a map from one context to another, it extends the map by adding some \lterm to the context.
The function \textsf{extb} takes a map from variables in one context to variables in another context and returns a map from variables in the first context extended with a type to variables in the second context extended with a type.
Again, this type will be the input type of the $\lambda$ abstraction in \textsf{renameb}.
Thus, the definition of simultaneous substitution is based on the definitions of \textsf{extsb}, \textsf{renameb} and \textsf{extb}.

\subsection{Weakening and Renaming}
Weakening and renaming becomes something to take into consideration in the formalisation. Two \lterms that are the same except for the context
are not exactly the same. An example to demonstrate this is the formal definition of \raiseembn in Agda,
where the use of \textsf{renameb} is required. Recall that \raiseembn was defined as follows:
\begin{flalign*}
 \hspace{20pt} \raiseemb{M} := \lamb{z}{\boxe{z}M} &&
\end{flalign*}
The term $M$ outside the function (in $\raiseemb{M}$) has a different context than the term $M$ inside the function of \raiseembn ($\lamb{z}{\boxe{z}M}$).
We attempt to give a typing derivation in \cref{fig:modal-implication}. We assume that term $M$ has type \textsf{B'} which can be derived from $\Gamma$.
However, we are stuck in the derivation since we do not have rules to discard assumptions. 
We see that we need to prove $\judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa B')}}{M : B'}$ instead of $\judgement{\Gamma}{M : B'}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \small
  \begin{prooftree}
    \def\extraVskip{5pt}
    
        \AxiomC{\phantom{A}}
        \RightLabel{Id$_\square$}
        \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa B')}}{\boxe{z} : \boxt{B} \fa B'}$}
      
        \AxiomC{\vdots}
        \noLine
        \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa B')}}{M : B'}$}
      \RightLabel{\impliese}
      \BinaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa B')}}{\boxe{z}M :B'}$}
    \RightLabel{\impliesi}
    \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma}{\lamb{z}{\boxe{z}M} : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa B')} \fa B'}$} % Conclusion
  \end{prooftree}
  \caption{Attempt of the typing derivation of $\lamb{z}{\boxe{z}M}$}
  \label{fig:modal-implication}
\end{figure}

Though we cannot immediately conclude the statement below, it does make sense that this is the case.
\[
\judgement{\Gamma}{M : B'} \mbox{ implies } \judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa \boxt{B'})}}{M : \boxt{B'}}
\]
We only add an extra assumption that we do not use, so we need a function that can handle this. 
This is where \textsf{renameb} comes in.
The function \textsf{renameb} "renames" the de Bruijn indices in the \lterms with a certain function and changes the context accordingly. 
In our case, we only need to rename the indices with \textsf{Sb} (\textsf{S} for \lab).

Let us show how this works in the formalisation.
Assume that we have a \lterm $M$ that has type $B'$ in context $\Gamma$, which is denoted by $M : \judgement{\Gamma}{B'}$ in the formalisation.
We use \textsf{renameb} in such a way that it returns a \lterm of the type $\judgement{\Gamma \ ,\textsf{b} \ \boxt{(\boxt{B \fa B'})}}{\boxt{B'}}$.
We achieve this by renaming with the function \textsf{Sb} as shown below. The resulting type is $\judgement{\Gamma \ ,\textsf{b} \ \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa \boxt{B'})}}{\boxt{B'}}$

\[
 \textsf{rename (Sb } \{\square B = \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa \boxt{B'})}\}) \ M
\]

Here, we need to give the implicit argument since \textsf{Sb} needs to know what the resulting context will be.
Now, we incorporate this into the definition of \raiseembn. 
Note that Agda can automatically infer the implicit arguments of \textsf{Sb} in the definition of \textsf{raise}.
