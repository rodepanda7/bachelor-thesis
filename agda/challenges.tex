\section{Challenges in Formalisation}

\subsection{De Bruijn Indices}
De Bruijn indeces, introduced by Nico G. de Bruijn, are a method to eliminate the need for variable names in the \lc \cite{DEBRUIJN1972381}.
This is useful because it makes the notion of \aeq irrelevant. 
We do not have to worry about variables with the same name that refer to different variables, or about variable capture, simplifying the formalisation in Agda.
The definition of substitution becomes simpler because of this.
This subsection first explains the concept of de Bruijn indices, and then shows how they are implemented in the formalisation.

The \lterms that use de Bruijn indices do not use variable names, but integers. We precede the integers with \# to prevent confusion if the index is higher than 9.
Without \#, $10$ could be confused with two indices $1$ and $0$ that occur next to each other. 
These integers represent the "distance" to the corresponding $\lambda$. 
\Cref{ex:deBruijn-closed} gives representations of \lterms without free variables, using de Bruijn indices.
These terms are said to be closed.

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:deBruijn-closed}
  Closed \lterms represented with \textbf{de Bruijn indices}:
  \begin{flalign*}
    & \lamb{x}{x}                                  \equiv \lamb{}{\#0}                        & \\
    & \lamb{x}{\lamb{y}{x}}                        \equiv \lamb{}{\lamb{}{\#1}}               & \\
    & \lamb{x}{\lamb{y}{y}}                        \equiv \lamb{}{\lamb{}{\#0}}               & \\
    & \lamb{x}{x}\lamb{y}{y}                       \equiv \lamb{}{\#0}\lamb{}{\#0}            & \\
  \end{flalign*}
\end{example}

Representing open \lterms (\lterms with free variables) using de Bruijn indices is a little more complex. 
First, we represent the \lterm with de Bruijn indices, ignoring the free variables.
Then, we give each free variable a unique index, that is larger than the largest index of the bound variables.
\Cref{ex:deBruijn-open} will demonstrate this.
\begin{example}
  Open \lterms represented with \textbf{de Bruijn indices}:
  \begin{flalign*}
    & x\lamb{y}{y}                                 \equiv x\lamb{}{0}      \equiv \#1\lamb{}{\#0}          & \\
    & x\lamb{y}{x}                                 \equiv x\lamb{}{x}      \equiv \#1\lamb{}{\#1}          & \\
    & \lamb{x}{\lamb{t}{x t (\lamb{s}{w})}wy}      \equiv \lamb{}{\lamb{}{\#1} \#0 (\lamb{}{w}) wy} \equiv \lamb{}{\lamb{}{\#1} \#0 (\lamb{}{\#4}) \#3\#2} & \\    
  \end{flalign*} 
\end{example}

In the formalisation, we will represent de Bruijn indices similar to how natural numbers are represented in Agda.
De Bruijn index 0 is represented as \textsf{Z}, de Bruijn index 1 as \textsf{S Z}, de Bruijn index 2 as \textsf{S (S Z)}, and so on.


\subsection{Well-typed Terms}

This subsection discusses the restriction to well-typed terms in the formalisation of \lab.
The restriction to well-typed terms is important because it avoids all complications that arise from ill typed terms. 
For example, in the context of evaluation, it makes sense to restrict to well-typed terms, 
because ill-typed terms do not have a normal form.
Evaluation is only defined for well-typed terms, and thus the restriction to well-typed terms aligns with the goal of the thesis.

In the formalisation, we use an intrinsically typed approach where terms are defined together with their types.
So \lterms in the formalisation are automatically associated with a type, and we can only construct well-typed terms.
This approach is also referred to as Church style typing, and was introduced by Alonzo Church in \cite{ALONZOCHURCH1940}.

The formalisation is based on the representation proposed by Thorsten AltenKirch and Bernhard Reus in \cite{THORSTENALTENKIRCH1999}.

\Cref{def:terms:agda} gives the formal definition of terms in the \lc.
The \lterms are defined by the context they are in and the type they have. For example,
$\Gamma \vdash \textsf{A}$ denotes the \lterms that have type \textsf{A} in the context $\Gamma$.
With the constructors $`$, $\lambdabar$ and $\cdot$, we can construct \lterms.'
The $`$ constructor is used to construct \lterms that are variables if we can provide a proof that the variable is in the context.
Abstractions are constructed with the $\lambdabar$ constructor, which takes a context with more than one variable,
and creates a new \lterm that has one less variable in the context with one. This new \lterm is the abstraction of the original \lterm.
The $\cdot$ constructor is used to construct applications of \lterms.
Given an abstraction and an argument for that abstraction, $\cdot$ constructs a new \lterm of the return type of the abstraction.
Examples of \lterms are given in \cref{ex:terms-agda}.
Note that we need to give the type of the \lterm, which is of the form $\Gamma \vdash \textsf{A}$,
and that we provide evidence for this type when constructing the \lterm.

\subsection{Substitution}
The next challenge in the formalisation is a correct definition of substitution.
This definition appeared to be the most complex part of the formalisation, and it caused the most problems with the proofs later in this thesis.
The definition of substitution is similar to the definition of substitution that is used in the chapter "De Bruijn indices" in the PLFA book \cite{plfa22.08}.
This definition is based on the definition by Conor McBride \cite{BENTON2012}, which gives a similar definition of substitution in Rocq.
In \cref{def:substitution:lab}, we only give the definition of substitution in Agda of \lterms in \lab, since the definition of substitution in \lc is exactly the same as \cite{plfa22.08}.
We first define parallel substitution, which is the substitution of a list of \lterms by another list of \lterms.
Then we define single substitution, which might be more familiar and is the substitution of a single \lterm by another \lterm.
Single substitution of the \lterm that occurs most recently in the context of $M$ by \lterm $N$ is denoted by $M [ N ]b$ in the formalisation of \lab.

\subsection{Weakening and Renaming}
Weakening and renaming becomes something to take into consideration in the formalisation. Two \lterms that are the same except for the contexts
are not exactly the same. An example to demonstrate this is the formal definition of \raiseembn in Agda,
where the use of \textsf{renameb} is required. Recall that \raiseembn was defined as follows:
\begin{flalign*}
  \hspace{20pt} \raiseemb{M} := \lamb{z}{\boxe{z}M} &&
\end{flalign*}
The term $M$ outside the function (in $\raiseemb{M}$) has a different context than the term $M$ inside the function of \raiseembn ($\lamb{z}{\boxe{z}M}$).
We attempt to give a typing derivation in \cref{fig:modal-implication}. We assume that term $M$ has type \textsf{B'} which can be derived from $\Gamma$.
However, we are stuck in the derivation since we do not have rules to discard assumptions. 
We see that we need to proof $\judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa B')}}{M : B'}$ instead of $\judgement{\Gamma}{M : B'}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \small
  \begin{prooftree}
    \def\extraVskip{5pt}
    
        \AxiomC{\phantom{A}}
        \RightLabel{Id$_\square$}
        \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{\boxt{B} \fa B'}}{\boxe{z} : \boxt{B} \fa B'}$}
      
        \AxiomC{\vdots}
        \noLine
        \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{\boxt{B} \fa B'}}{M : B'}$}
      \RightLabel{\impliese}
      \BinaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{\boxt{B} \fa B'}}{\boxe{z}M :B'}$}
    \RightLabel{\impliesi}
    \UnaryInfC{$\judgement{\Gamma}{\lamb{z}{\boxe{z}M} : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa B')} \fa B'}$} % Conclusion
  \end{prooftree}
  \caption{The attempt of the typing derivation of $\lamb{z}{\boxe{z}M}$}
  \label{fig:modal-implication}
\end{figure}

However, it makes sense that $\judgement{\Gamma}{M : B'} \mbox{ implies } \judgement{\Gamma , z : \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa \boxt{B'})}}{M : \boxt{B'}}$.
We only add an extra assumption that we do not use, so we need a function that can handle this. 
This is where \textsf{renameb} comes in.
The function \textsf{renameb} "renames" the de Bruijn indices in the \lterms with a certain function and changes the context accordingly. 
In our case, we need to rename the indices with \textsf{Sb} (\textsf{S} for \lab).

Let us show how this works in the formalisation.
We have a \lterm $M$ that has type $B'$ in context $\Gamma$, which is denoted by $M : \judgement{\Gamma}{B'}$ in the formalisation.
Therefore, we use \textsf{renameb} in such a way that it returns a \lterm of the type $\judgement{\Gamma \ ,\textsf{b} \ \boxt{(\boxt{B \fa B'})}}{\boxt{B'}}$.
We achieve this by renaming with the function \textsf{Sb} as shown below. The resulting type is $\judgement{\Gamma \ ,\textsf{b} \ \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa \boxt{B'})}}{\boxt{B'}}$

\[
  \textsf{rename (Sb } \{\square B = \boxt{(\boxt{B} \fa \boxt{B'})}\}) \ M
\]

Here, we needed to give the implicit argument since \textsf{Sb} needs to know what to the resulting context should be.
Now, we incorporate this into the definition of \raiseembn. 
Note that Agda can automatically infer the implicit arguments of \textsf{Sb} in the definition of \textsf{raise}.
